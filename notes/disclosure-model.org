#+title: Disclosing data to an individual: what a MinoCrab contract can and cannot promise

The executable half is =crates/minocrab-contracts/src/disclosure.rs= and =crates/minocrab-contracts/tests/disclosure.rs= (8 tests, green).

The question as dmd put it: can a smart contract disclose data to an individual person — some encryption their key can decrypt privately — in the spirit of DAML's privacy model, not its implementation, but its ability to make sure people can see data within their purview?

* 1. The short answer

Yes to the cryptography, no to the architecture, and the gap between those two is the whole interesting part.

A MinoCrab contract can, today, with primitives already in the eDSL, compute in-circuit a ciphertext that only the holder of a named Jubjub secret key can open, and PROVE that the ciphertext is a well-formed encryption of the very value the circuit committed to elsewhere, addressed to the very key the ledger has registered for that account. The recipient opens it with no proof, no circuit and no transaction — one scalar multiplication and two SHA-256s over a ledger read. =tests/disclosure.rs::the_recipient_recovers_the_value_and_nobody_else_does= is that claim, executed: the wallet side is native Rust against Midnight's own off-circuit helpers, so the two implementations agree about Midnight rather than about each other.

What cannot be had is the part of DAML's model that is not cryptographic at all. DAML hides data by NOT TRANSMITTING IT. Midnight hides data by transmitting ciphertext to everyone, permanently. Those are different enough that four of DAML's guarantees do not survive the translation, and one of them (§3, forward secrecy) is strictly worse here than a comment about "encryption" would suggest.

* 2. What DAML actually gives you, and which half of it is crypto

Stated from knowledge, NOT verified against a Canton source tree — dmd should correct anything here before it is quoted outward.

A DAML template names =signatory= and =observer= parties; their union is the contract's stakeholders. A choice names =controller= parties. The ledger model says a party learns about exactly the contracts it is a stakeholder of, plus what it is divulged by being an informee of a transaction that used some other contract. Canton implements this by splitting a transaction into VIEWS and delivering each view only to its informees; a participant that is not an informee of a view receives a blinded hash, not a payload. There is encryption in there — view payloads go through the sequencer encrypted to the informee participants — but the sequencer is an operated service with retention policy, not a permanent public replicated log, and a non-informee never holds the key and never holds the blob either.

So DAML's privacy decomposes into four separate promises, and they are worth separating because they translate differently:

1. CONFIDENTIALITY. A non-stakeholder cannot read the payload. Cryptographic, and it translates.
2. METADATA PRIVACY. A non-stakeholder cannot see that the contract exists, who its stakeholders are, or how many there are. Architectural. Does not translate; see §4.
3. EXCLUSIVITY. The set of parties who can read is exactly the stakeholder set. Architectural, and unprovable here; see §6.
4. AUTHORITY. Only signatories can create, only controllers can exercise. Translates, in a different shape; see §6.

Plus one thing DAML does NOT give that Midnight gives for free: every reader sees the same ledger, so there is no projection-consistency question at all.

* 3. The row table

| DAML guarantee                                  | here                                                        | verdict                   |
|-------------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------+---------------------------|
| stakeholder reads the payload                   | ECDH memo to a registered Jubjub key, opened natively       | works, tested             |
| payload bound to what the transaction committed | the ciphertext is computed in-circuit from the same wire    | works, and BETTER         |
| non-stakeholder cannot read it                  | IND-CPA under ODH, 2^-120 hiding slack at =Uint<128>=       | works, cryptographic      |
| only signatories can create                     | assert a signature or knowledge of a secret in-circuit      | expressible, not a primitive |
| all stakeholders see the same thing             | one global ledger                                           | free                      |
| non-stakeholder cannot see the contract EXISTS  | every memo is a public ledger row                           | NO                        |
| non-stakeholder cannot see WHO the parties are  | the registry read publishes the recipient (§4)              | mitigable, not free       |
| exactly the stakeholders can read               | the sender may encrypt to extra keys; non-disclosure is unprovable | NO, fundamentally  |
| recipient is guaranteed to receive it           | well-formedness is provable, delivery and liveness are not  | NO                        |
| a key compromise in 2031 reveals nothing from 2026 | every ciphertext is permanently public                   | NO, and WORSE than DAML   |

The last row deserves its own paragraph because it is the one a reader is most likely to get wrong. In Canton, a participant that was never an informee never held the blob; compromising its keys years later yields nothing about transactions it was not party to. Here, the ciphertext is in permanent public chain state, so a leaked =ek= retroactively opens every memo ever addressed to it, and an adversary can harvest now and decrypt later — including after a cryptographically relevant quantum computer, since Jubjub ECDH is not post-quantum. Key rotation per epoch limits the blast radius of a future compromise but does not help the harvested past, and the recipient must keep old keys to re-read their own history, so there is no forward secrecy to be had without giving up readability. THE HONEST CLAIM IS CONFIDENTIALITY, NOT FORWARD SECRECY.

* 4. What the ledger publishes, and the three ways to publish less

As built:

#+begin_src text
export ledger keys:  Map<Bytes<32>, (Field, Field)>;  // account -> encode(pk)
export ledger memos: List<(Field, Field, Field)>;     // encode(E) ‖ ct
#+end_src

=memos= is ONE global append-only list, deliberately, not a per-recipient =Map<account, List<..>>= as OpenZeppelin's confidential token uses. A per-recipient map publishes who received a disclosure and how many and when — exactly the metadata DAML's view projection withholds. A single list publishes only that a disclosure happened, and costs the recipient a trial-decryption scan: one =ec_mul= and two SHA-256s per memo, off-chain. That is the Zcash model and it scales to millions of memos per scan only with Zcash's own trick, a one-byte VIEW TAG published in the clear, cutting 255/256 of the work at the price of bucketing recipients 256 ways.

The leak that remains, and it is a real one: =disclose_to= reads =keys[account]= to bind the supplied point to the registered one, and A MAP LOOKUP'S KEY IS AN IMPACT OPERAND. There is no private map read in Impact — the key goes into the public transcript. So the circuit publishes which account is being disclosed to, and the typed declaration says so (=RegisteredAccount=). Three ways out, none free:

1. DROP THE REGISTRY READ. Have the sender witness the recipient's key instead of reading it. The recipient disappears from the transcript, and so does the binding: nothing then stops a sender from addressing a memo to themselves and calling it a disclosure to Alice. Trades guarantee (3) for guarantee (2).
2. ONE-TIME ADDRESSES. Derive a per-call recipient key from a shared secret and a diversifier, so no stored key is ever read and no two memos to the same person look related. This is Sapling/Orchard, and it is the real answer; it costs another curve operation and a scanning protocol.
3. COMMIT ON-CHAIN, DELIVER OFF-CHAIN. Publish only a commitment to the value and hand the opening to the recipient over any channel. Cheapest, leaks least, and no permanent ciphertext to harvest — which makes it the design CLOSEST to DAML, since DAML also does not put payloads in a shared log. The on-chain part proves consistency; delivery is somebody else's problem, as it is in Canton.

(3) is underrated and should be the baseline anyone compares against. The reason to do (1) or (2) instead is that they need no off-chain channel at all.

* 5. The sharpest edge: =e= must be fresh AND secret

=mask = KDF(pk^e)= is a ONE-TIME PAD KEY, and it has two preconditions the circuit cannot check.

UNIQUE. A repeated =e= to the same recipient repeats the mask, and subtracting the two ciphertexts recovers both plaintexts.

SECRET. =pk= is public. Anyone who learns =e= recomputes =S = pk^e=, hence the mask, and opens the ciphertext. So a unique but PREDICTABLE =e= — a counter, a block height, a public nonce — breaks confidentiality completely, for every memo, retroactively. This is strictly stronger than exponential ElGamal's requirement, where reuse merely leaks plaintext equality.

A circuit sees a field element. It cannot tell a high-entropy witness from =block_height + 1=. =ephemeral_scalar= expands a =Bytes<32>= seed with a tag, which gives uniqueness across siblings in one call for free, and gives nothing at all about entropy or secrecy. THE SEED MUST BE A WITNESS THE SENDER'S WALLET GENERATES FROM A CSPRNG AND NEVER PUBLISHES. If an exploration of this ever reaches a real deployment, this is the line to put in front of a reviewer first, because it is the one where a plausible-looking implementation is completely broken and every test still passes.

* 6. What the circuit proves, versus what it merely does

PROVES: the memo is a well-formed hashed-ElGamal encryption, under a non-degenerate ephemeral key, of a value the circuit range-constrained to 128 bits, to the public key the ledger has registered for the named account. A sender cannot address a memo to a key of their choosing and have it counted as a disclosure to that account — =a_memo_cannot_be_addressed_to_an_unregistered_key= is that, failing on the binding's =constrain_eq=.

MERELY DOES: delivery. Nothing makes the recipient's wallet scan, nothing makes the sender's transaction land, and a recipient who loses their key loses their history. DAML's model has an operator and a protocol behind delivery; a ZK contract has neither.

CANNOT DO AT ALL: exclusivity. The sender can encrypt the same value to a second key in the same transaction or a later one, and no proof system can establish that they did not — non-disclosure is not a statement about the transcript. If exclusivity matters, it has to come from the sender being the only party who could know the value (a commitment they alone opened), not from the disclosure mechanism.

AUTHORITY, which is DAML's other half: expressible, and this repo already has the pieces. A =controller= is "assert, in-circuit, that the caller holds a secret / produced a signature" — =secp256k1_ecdsa_verify= and =own_public_key= are both in =minocrab-std=. What is missing versus DAML is that authority there is a LEDGER-MODEL concept checked uniformly, and here it is an assertion each circuit has to remember to write. That asymmetry is worth a note of its own if the exploration continues.

REGISTRATION, AND THE TWO HOLES THAT ARE NOT ONE HOLE. The first draft of this note treated "=register= does not prove possession of =ek=" as a single defect with a single cheap fix. It is two, they need different fixes, and only one of them is now closed — a distinction worth keeping, because conflating them is how "we check the key" comes to sound like "accounts are safe".
- A KEY THE REGISTRANT DOES NOT HOLD. CLOSED 2026-10-13: =register= witnesses =ek= and binds =ec_mul_generator(ek)= to the supplied =pk= limb for limb. This mattered more than "proof of possession" makes it sound — without it anyone could point an account at a key whose secret is lost or never existed, and since the registry entry is the only thing =disclose_to= binds against, every disclosure to that account would be undecryptable by ANYONE and no later transaction could undo it. A denial of service, not just an impersonation risk. Cost: =register= 157 → 840 rows, k 8 → 10, which is one generator multiplication and two =test_eq= and is paid once per account. Gated by =a_key_the_registrant_does_not_hold_cannot_be_registered=, which also registers the same point with its OWN secret so the refusal is the proof talking and not the shape of the call. The identity point still registers, with =ek = 0=, because =g^0 = identity= really is a proof of possession for it — it dies at =derive_shared= instead, which is where it should.
- SOMEONE ELSE'S ACCOUNT UNDER A KEY THEY DO HOLD. CLOSED 2026-10-14 by §12 option 2, which dmd chose. =register= takes NO account argument: the id is =account_of_caller=, a commitment to a witnessed caller secret, so the only entry a caller can write is the one their own secret derives. Squatting is not expensive now, it is INEXPRESSIBLE — there is no value to supply that names somebody else's account. Pinned by =the_account_is_derived_from_the_caller_not_chosen=, which registers two callers against one state and checks that Alice's entry still binds her key and refuses Mallory's.

  WHICH ALSO CLOSED A HOLE THE FIRST DRAFT HAD NOT SPOTTED. Before option 2 this was worse than "whoever registers first owns it": =register= is a bare =Map.insert= with no non-membership check, and an Impact =ins= overwrites, so a later call took over a LIVE registration — Mallory did not have to get there first. That is why §12 option 1 (a =member= check) was going to be mandatory. Deriving the account dissolves it instead: the entry a caller can overwrite is only ever their own, so the overwrite is key ROTATION, which a registry needs anyway — a recipient who loses =ek= would otherwise be locked out forever. =re_registering_rotates_the_callers_own_key= is that, end to end. Option 1 is therefore NOT needed, and a =member= check would now be actively wrong, since it would forbid rotation.

* 7. Cost, measured

=tests/disclosure.rs::the_row_and_k_table=, this worktree, 2026-10-11:

| circuit    |  k |  rows |
|------------+----+-------|
| register   | 10 |  1006 |
| discloseTo | 14 | 12796 |

Region attribution of =discloseTo=: 64.7% =ecdh derive shared= (~8049 rows — the two unavoidable scalar multiplications, =E = g^e= and =S = pk^e=, at roughly 4k each), 31.9% top level (the Impact ops, the =Uint<128>= range constraint, the registry binding), 3.2% randomness expansion, and the two SHA-256 hashes essentially free. SO A DISCLOSURE IS A CURVE COST, NOT A HASH COST, which is the opposite of most circuits in this repo.

=the_marginal_cost_of_an_extra_recipient= measures the marginal recipient at 8290 rows. That is TWO curve operations, because =encrypt= recomputes =E = g^e= per recipient; one ephemeral can serve all of them (=E= once, then =S_i = pk_i^e=), so a multi-recipient primitive would pay about half. It is deliberately not written that way yet — hoisting the ephemeral out of =derive_shared= puts it outside the guarded primitive, which is exactly the factoring mistake §9 is about.

The number that matters for the DAML comparison: AN OBSERVER IS FREE IN DAML AND COSTS ~4–8k ROWS HERE. A contract with a handful of auditors is fine; one with a stakeholder list that grows with its users is not, and would want a different shape entirely (a shared key the contract rotates, or (3) from §4).

* 8. The rungs, in order

1. REGISTRATION, BOTH HALVES. DONE: possession of =ek= 2026-10-13 and the caller-derived account 2026-10-14 (§6). =register= 157 → 1006 rows, k 8 → 10, for one generator multiplication, one transient hash and two =test_eq=. What is still NOT proven at registration is that any particular counterparty OUGHT to disclose to this account — that is §12 option 3's issuer signature, and it is a policy question a contract answers, not a defect here.
2. A VIEW TAG. One clear byte of the KDF output per memo, cutting the recipient's scan by 255/256. Costs nothing in-circuit and buckets recipients 256 ways; the trade is worth writing down before choosing.
3. ONE-TIME ADDRESSES. §4's option (2) — the real fix for recipient privacy, and the biggest single piece of work here.
4. A MULTI-RECIPIENT PRIMITIVE that hoists the ephemeral while keeping the weak-input guards inside the only function that can produce a shared secret. §7's factor of two, done without reintroducing §9's mistake.
5. =fieldKdf=, if anything ever needs to disclose a value wider than =Uint<128>= — a record, a hash, a commitment. RFC 9380 =hash_to_field= with an MGF1 expander. §9 says why this is not optional.
6. WIDER PAYLOADS. Either n field pads (cheap, n more =add=s, same DH) or a witness-side AEAD with an in-circuit commitment binding it (very cheap in rows, and the binding is what keeps it honest). The corpus has precedent for the latter: =zerocash= declares =witness context$encrypt(pk: Opaque<"Uint8Array">)= with =ledger ciphertexts: List<Opaque<"Uint8Array">>= (notes/opaque-bridging.org §1). THE CATCH, and it is the whole reason the in-circuit path exists: a witness-side ciphertext is NOT bound to the value the circuit committed to, so a malicious sender can publish garbage and the proof still verifies — a stakeholder can be denied their view, silently.
7. AUTHORITY AS A LIBRARY SHAPE rather than a per-circuit assertion, if the DAML comparison is to go anywhere.

* 9. Upstream is fixing two things, and we should not re-make either

Both are OPEN PRs on =OpenZeppelin/compact-contracts=, read 2026-10-11, neither merged — so the vendored corpus copy AND upstream =main= still have both.

** #1110, "derive ElGamal's mask through Ecdh" (3/3)

=EcdhMask.encrypt= and =ElGamal.encryptPoint= each carried their own Diffie-Hellman step and their own copy of the two weak-input guards, so the guards could drift apart. The fix routes both through one =Ecdh.deriveShared=.

=disclosure.rs= takes the lesson further than Compact can. =derive_shared= is the only DH, and it returns a =SharedSecret= whose inner field is private to the module, so there is NO WAY to obtain a shared secret without passing the guards — a second encryption path would have nothing to hand =kdf=. In Compact the factoring is a convention a contributor can bypass by writing their own =ecMul=, which is precisely how the duplication arose; here it is a type error. This is the =compile errors over panics= rule applied to a cryptographic invariant, and it is a nice datapoint for TRUST.md.

** #913, "hash to field and a uniform EcdhMask pad" (2/3) — the one that is a real leak

=ct = m + kdf(S)= with =kdf(S) < 2^248= NEVER WRAPS. So a plaintext that reaches bit 248 publishes its top bits in the clear, and not statistically — TOTALLY. =mask < 2^248= cannot carry into bit 248, so bit 248 of the ciphertext IS bit 248 of the plaintext, for every pad, from a single observation. =the_248_bit_pad_cannot_hide_a_wide_value= demonstrates exactly that over 32 real KDF outputs, and demonstrates the other side too: at =Uint<128>= the same observation determines nothing, because bit 248 of the plaintext is zero whatever it is.

OZ's own =encrypt= is safe — its =value= is a =Uint<128>=, hidden with ~2^-120 slack — but the module is described as masking "a single field element" and its =decrypt= returns a =Field=, which invites a field-sized plaintext into a pad that cannot hide one. Their fix is =fieldKdf=.

WHERE WE STAND: safe by the TYPE and not by the comment. =encrypt= takes =Uint<128>=, range-constrained in-circuit, and there is deliberately no =encrypt_field= to reach for. That is the whole mitigation, and it is why rung 5 is a prerequisite for rung 6 rather than a nice-to-have.

* 10. MinoCrab-specific findings, collected

(a) A =JubjubPoint= is =Cell=-ONLY in a ledger slot. Its limbs come out of an =encode= instruction and ZKIR has no in-circuit inverse, so =LedgerRepr::from_limbs= cannot rebuild one and =LedgerMap<_, JubjubPoint>= does not exist. The way round is to store the two =encode= limbs as plain fields (=PointLimbs=), have the sender supply the typed point as an argument, and bind the two. Off-circuit =decode_offcircuit= reverses it, which is all a wallet needs. This is why OZ's =Map<Bytes<32>, List<EcdhMask_Ciphertext>>= has no direct translation.

(b) THE VENDORED OZ CRYPTO MODULES DO NOT COMPILE under the pinned compactc, and our note on that was wrong until it was checked. =corpus/compile-report.tsv=: =EcdhMask.compact line 148 char 22: no compatible function named ecMulGenerator is in scope … supplied (Field), declared (JubjubScalar)=. The vendored rev (5f3ecd4, v0.3.0-alpha.2) passes a =Field=; compactc 0.34.0 wants a =JubjubScalar=. Upstream =main= (v0.4.0-alpha.6) has since changed the signature, so this is OUR CORPUS BEING STALE, not upstream being broken. Worth a =corpus/update.sh= run. Consequence for us: there is no compiled artifact, so no differential — the native wallet in the test suite is the oracle instead, and it is arguably a better one, being a different implementation path rather than the same compiler twice.

(c) ZKIR HAS =jubjub_scalar_from_native=, which REDUCES mod ℓ. Compact does not expose it, which is why OZ reaches for =degradeToTransient(persistentHash(..))= — truncate to 248 bits, which is under ℓ, so always in range. =ephemeral_scalar= keeps the truncation in front of the reduction so a seed yields bit-for-bit the same scalar OZ's module yields, and the reduction makes range-correctness stop depending on 248 happening to be below ℓ.

(d) POINT EQUALITY must go through =encode= and two =test_eq=. =test_eq= is a field-and-=Bytes<32>= instruction; a point is neither.

(e) THE JUBJUB IDENTITY IS AFFINE =(0, 1)=, so the weak-input guard can compare against two immediates instead of spending =ecMulGenerator(0)= on a compile-time-known value as Compact must. Measured saving 657 rows (13453 → 12796), k unchanged at 14 — less than a general scalar multiplication, because the cost model already knew the operand was zero. Pinned by =the_identity_point_encodes_to_zero_one=, so the constant is a test and not a belief.

(f) THE NATIVE PER-RECIPIENT CHANNEL EXISTS AND A CONTRACT MAY NOT USE IT. A zswap =Output= carries =ciphertext: Option<Sp<CoinCiphertext>>=, built as =CoinCiphertext::new(rng, coin, epk)= to the recipient's encryption key — Midnight already has a per-recipient encrypted channel in consensus. But =MalformedOffer::ContractSentCiphertext= ("contract was sent an output with a ciphertext") makes a contract-addressed output carrying one MALFORMED, and the payload is a =CoinInfo= regardless, not arbitrary data. So the chain's own channel is user-to-user value transfer only, and a contract wanting to disclose to a person has to build §1's path. This is the single most useful thing to know before starting.

(g) =PointLimbs::disclose_as= produces TWO disclosure records under one label, not one record of two wires, because =disclose= is per-wire. Anything reading =Compiled3::disclosures= by label has to collect all matching records. Cost a debugging round in =tests/disclosure.rs=; worth knowing for any multi-wire typed disclosure.

* 12. Stopping account squatting: the options, and what each costs (2026-10-14)

dmd asked what it would take. The question is really "where does the meaning of =account= come from?", and the four answers differ in who gets to say. The primitives for all of them are already in the tree — =LedgerMap::member=, =own_public_key=, =secp256k1_ecdsa_verify=, =common::commitment_transient= — so none of this is blocked on the eDSL.

1. A NON-MEMBERSHIP CHECK, i.e. make registration a one-shot. =c.assert(not(is_true(DISCLOSURE.keys.member(c, &account).stale(c))))=, the same guard =Stream::insert= already uses for its keys. This does NOT stop the land-grab — first writer still wins — but it closes the TAKEOVER half completely, and it is one =member= read plus an assert, which is the cheapest thing in this list by an order of magnitude. It composes with every option below and should be done whatever else is chosen. The thing to decide alongside it is ROTATION: once an account is one-shot, a recipient who loses their key is locked out forever, so the honest shape is =rotate(account, new_pk)= gated on possession of the CURRENTLY registered key, which is the same curve operation =register= now pays.
2. DERIVE THE ACCOUNT FROM THE CALLER. Stop taking =account= as an argument and compute it — =account = H(own_public_key(c))=, or the vault's =commitment_transient= over a witnessed caller secret. Then "Alice's account" MEANS "the holder of this key" by construction and squatting is not a thing that can be expressed: there is no id to claim. Cheapest real fix (one hash; =own_public_key= is a kernel read, not a curve operation) and the one to reach for by default. The cost is expressiveness: the account id is now a function of a Midnight key, so it cannot be an externally-chosen identifier — no KYC reference, no EVM address, no issuer-assigned customer number.
3. AN AUTHORITY SIGNATURE OVER =(account, pk)=. Store an issuer key in the ledger and have =register= verify an ECDSA signature over the pair, so the issuer decides which account ids exist and who holds each one. This is the option that fits the RWA-issuer framing the README uses: there the gatekeeper is not a cost to be apologised for, it is the product — an issuer who must approve every registration is exactly an issuer who knows who their holders are. It keeps arbitrary external account ids, which is the thing option 2 gives up. The price is an in-circuit =secp256k1_ecdsa_verify=, the same primitive the vault's attestation path pays and the reason its settles are ECDSA-floored; paid once per account, it is affordable, but =register= stops being an 840-row circuit.
4. NO REGISTRY AT ALL. §4's option (2), one-time addresses: derive a per-call recipient key from a shared secret and a diversifier, so no stored key is ever read and there is nothing to squat. It also fixes the recipient-privacy leak in the same stroke, which neither 2 nor 3 touches. It is the biggest piece of work here and needs a scanning protocol.

DECIDED 2026-10-14: dmd took OPTION 2. As built, =account_of_caller= is =upgradeFromTransient(transientHash([pad(32, "disclosure:acct:"), sk]))= over a witnessed caller secret, +166 rows on =register= (840 → 1006), k unchanged at 10.

Two things the choice settled that the list above had wrong or open:
- OPTION 1 IS NOT NEEDED, and would now be harmful. The =member= check was mandatory only because a caller-chosen account could be overwritten by anyone; once the id is caller-derived, the only entry a caller can overwrite is their own, so the overwrite IS the rotation option 1 would have had to add back. Adding a non-membership check now would forbid rotation and lock out anyone who loses =ek=.
- DERIVED FROM A SECRET, NOT FROM =own_public_key=, which is the cheaper way to do option 2 and the wrong one. Hashing the caller's public key is equally squat-proof, but anyone who knew that public key could compute the account id, so every disclosure to it would be publicly attributable to its owner — it would hand away by construction the one piece of recipient privacy §4 is still trying to keep. A commitment to a secret is a stable pseudonym nobody can compute from public data; the recipient tells senders their account id, which they had to do anyway. The domain pad is =disclosure:acct:= and NOT the vault's =vault:user:=, because the vault discloses its user commitment, so sharing the pad would make the two contracts linkable for anyone holding the same =sk=.

What option 2 does NOT fix, and §13 is the reason to care: the account id is still a PUBLIC map key at =disclose_to=, so the registry read still publishes which account a disclosure is addressed to. Option 2 is about who may claim an account, not about who can see it being used. Only option 4 touches that.

* 13. Composing a disclosure with a shielded UTXO: what it costs transaction privacy (2026-10-14)

dmd asked whether this breaks transaction privacy under a shielded UTXO. Short answer: the VALUE stays confidential, every piece of METADATA around it does not, and the account id is the worst of it. Precisely: =Disclosure= has no coin arm — it moves no value at all — so nothing here breaks shielding on its own. The cost appears when =disclose_to= is composed into a contract that also moves shielded value, which is the only way anyone would use it.

WHAT STILL HOLDS. Hashed ElGamal is IND-CPA under ODH, so a non-recipient learns nothing about =value= — not its magnitude, not whether two memos carry the same number (§5's freshness obligation is what buys the second half). An observer does not learn the shielded coin's owner, its nullifier linkage, or its amount from the memo. The cryptography is not where this leaks.

WHAT BREAKS, in descending order of how much it matters:
- THE RECIPIENT IS PUBLIC. =account= is a map lookup key, hence an Impact operand, hence in the public transcript (§4). So a transaction carrying a disclosure announces WHO it was disclosed to. Compose that with a shielded transfer and the observer learns that this account is a party to this transaction — exactly the linkage shielding exists to prevent. WHO it burns depends on the pattern: disclosing to a COUNTERPARTY burns the counterparty, and is the bad case; disclosing to an ISSUER, as the README's RWA framing does, burns only the issuer, who is the same party on every such transaction and already knows. That is the difference between leaking a user and leaking an asset class, and it is why the RWA shape is the defensible use and a peer-to-peer "disclose to the person I paid" is not.
- EXISTENCE, COUNT AND TIMING. =memos= is ONE global append-only list and each call appends exactly one row, so if a shielded transfer carries a disclosure then the memo list is a PUBLIC COUNTER of otherwise-shielded transactions, with block timing. Amounts and parties stay hidden; "how much is going on, and when" does not.
- THE ANONYMITY SET SPLITS. A Midnight call reveals its contract and entry point, so a shielded transfer WITH a disclosure attached is distinguishable from one without. Every shielded transaction now sits in the smaller of two sets rather than the whole. This is the ordinary unlinkability-analysis concern and it applies here with full force.
- IT IS RETROACTIVE, which is the one a reader is most likely to underrate. §3's forward-secrecy row says every ciphertext is permanently public; under shielding that means a key compromise in 2031 reveals the AMOUNTS of transactions a user shielded in 2026 believing no amount would ever leak. Harvest-now-decrypt-later applies, and Jubjub ECDH is not post-quantum. A shielded transfer's own note ciphertext is in far better company.

THE COIN MESSAGE FIELD IS NOT AN ALTERNATIVE TO THIS, and it is worth being exact about why, because the first draft of this section got the direction of the restriction backwards. Read in the pinned ledger (=zswap/src/verify.rs=, =structure.rs=, rev =a01a1ea=):
- =MalformedOffer::ContractSentCiphertext= fires on =(contract_address: Some(_), ciphertext: Some(_))= — an output whose RECIPIENT is a contract may not carry a ciphertext. It is not a ban on outputs a contract SENDS, which is what "ContractSentCiphertext" reads like and what this note briefly claimed. Finding (f) had it right ("a contract-addressed output"); the paraphrase here did not.
- The payload is a =CoinInfo= in a fixed =CoinCiphertext { c: EmbeddedGroupAffine, ciph: [Fr; 6] }=, built OFF-circuit by =CoinCiphertext::new(rng, coin, pk)=. So it is six field elements of coin metadata produced by a wallet with an RNG, not a slot for arbitrary data and not something a circuit computes.
So the two channels do not compete, they cover disjoint needs. For "tell the person I just paid about the coin I paid them", the coin ciphertext is STRICTLY BETTER and should be used: it rides inside the shielded set, creates no public ledger row, and names no recipient. For "a contract discloses a value it proved something about, to a named party", the coin field cannot express it at all — wrong payload type, and nothing binds its plaintext to what a circuit computed, which is the one property §6 says this module's in-circuit path buys. That gap is why this path exists, and §13's leaks are what it costs.

NOT ESTABLISHED, and it would need checking before anyone leans on it: whether a CONTRACT-ORIGINATED output to a USER key can carry a ciphertext in practice, and who would construct it. The =well_formed= check above permits it (=contract_address= is =None= for a user-addressed output) and the output proof binds =ciphertext_to_field(ciph)= into its statement, but the construction path in =zswap/src/construct.rs= is the wallet's, and nothing here exercises a contract filling that field. Treat it as an open question, not a capability.

SO: "can you do private transactions with this?" Value privacy on a contract comes from SHIELDED COINS, which contracts already have (the vault mints and sends shielded tokens) and which this module is irrelevant to. This module buys one thing shielding does not: a named party can READ a specific value, and the ciphertext is bound in-circuit to what the transaction committed. Reach for it only when that reading requirement is real, and accept §13's metadata cost; if any off-chain channel is available, §4's option (3) — commit on-chain, deliver the opening off-chain — gets the same binding with none of the permanent ciphertext and none of the public recipient, and is the better default.

WHAT WOULD FIX WHICH. §4's option (2), one-time addresses, is the only one that removes the public account id, and it fixes the anonymity-set split for the recipient at the same time — it is the real answer and the biggest piece of work. §4's option (3), commit on-chain and deliver off-chain, leaks least of all and is the closest thing to not breaking shielding, because there is no permanent ciphertext to harvest. A view tag (rung 2) is a SCAN optimisation and makes privacy very slightly worse, not better — it buckets recipients 256 ways; it is not a mitigation and should not be mistaken for one. Nothing cheap fixes the existence-and-count leak; that needs decoy memos or batching, i.e. paying rows for padding.
